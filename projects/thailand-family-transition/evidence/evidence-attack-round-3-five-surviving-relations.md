# Evidence Attack Round 3 — five relations that survived global theory collision

## Purpose

After the global theory collision, most broad claims were absorbed by established frameworks. This round attacks only the five relations that remained potentially useful to the paper.

The question for each relation is:

\[
\text{Does the evidence support the relation itself, only a narrower version, or not yet?}
\]

Decision labels:

- **RETAIN** — evidence supports the relation at the stated scope.
- **RETAIN / NARROW** — a narrower relation survives.
- **OPEN** — plausible and testable, but not established.
- **REMOVE** — current evidence does not justify keeping it.

---

# 1. Family-transition contact as a gateway to later support

## Working relation before attack

\[
TransitionContact
\stackrel{?}{\longrightarrow}
LaterSupportAccess
\]

The strongest marriage-specific version is:

\[
PremaritalEducation
\stackrel{?}{\longrightarrow}
LaterRelationshipHelpSeeking
\]

---

## Positive evidence

### Williamson et al. 2014 — gateway association

A cross-sectional study of 2,126 married individuals found that premarital education was associated with a higher likelihood of later couples counselling. The association was stronger among African American participants and participants with lower income or education.

Source:

- Williamson HC, Trail TE, Bradbury TN, Karney BR. *Does premarital education decrease or increase couples' later help-seeking?* J Fam Psychol. 2014. PMID: 24294929.

### Williamson et al. 2018 — longitudinal replication

Five waves of self-report data from 431 ethnically diverse newlywed couples in lower-income neighbourhoods were used to examine later help-seeking.

Key observations:

- 36.3% of couples reporting premarital education later received therapy versus 23.1% without premarital education;
- among spouses who had considered therapy, follow-through was higher if they had received premarital education;
- among couples who eventually received therapy, those with premarital education did so earlier;
- several effects differed by sex, with some help-seeking-attitude effects evident among wives but not husbands.

Source:

- Williamson HC, Hammett JF, Ross JM, Karney BR, Bradbury TN. *Premarital Education and Later Relationship Help-seeking*. J Fam Psychol. 2018;32(2):276–281. DOI: 10.1037/fam0000383. PMID: 29658765.

### Transition-to-parenthood access-window evidence

Petch et al. approached couples during the transition to parenthood:

- 53% of eligible couples agreed to participate;
- 80% of participants had never previously accessed couple relationship education;
- approximately one third had three or more risk factors for later relationship distress;
- low education predicted dropout.

Source:

- Petch J, Halford WK, Creedy DK, Gamble J. *Couple relationship education at the transition to parenthood: a window of opportunity to reach high-risk couples*. Fam Process. 2012;51(4):498–511. DOI: 10.1111/j.1545-5300.2012.01420.x.

---

## Strongest attack

The 2018 premarital-help-seeking study explicitly states that it is **correlational, not randomized**.

Premarital-education participants may already differ in:

- comfort with help-seeking;
- psychological-mindedness;
- previous positive intervention experiences;
- religious/community participation;
- motivation for relationship maintenance.

The study also measured premarital education crudely and could not identify programme content or delivery setting.

A related low-income sample analysis found that premarital education predicted later therapy in unadjusted analysis, but after adding husbands' and wives' religious attendance, only wives' religious attendance was significant in that specific model.

Source:

- Williamson HC et al. *Barriers and Facilitators of Relationship Help-Seeking Among Low-Income Couples*. J Fam Psychol / PMC6389366.

---

## What survives

Evidence supports:

\[
PremaritalEducation
\leftrightarrow
LaterRelationshipHelpSeeking
\]

as a replicated association.

It does **not** yet establish:

\[
PremaritalContact
\rightarrow
LaterFamilySupportAccess
\]

for mental health, fertility care, parenting, violence services, child development and social support generally.

Nor does it establish that premarital education itself causes the gateway effect.

---

## Revised relation

\[
PremaritalContact
\stackrel{?}{\longrightarrow}
LaterHelpSeeking
\]

with the first empirical test restricted to:

\[
LaterRelationshipHelpSeeking
\]

before expanding across service domains.

Potential mediators to test:

\[
Gateway
=
f(
HelpSeekingFamiliarity,\,
ServiceKnowledge,\,
StigmaReduction,\,
ReferralMemory,\,
Trust
)
\]

---

## Prospective falsifier

Randomize or quasi-randomize a navigation/help-seeking component at a premarital contact.

If later:

\[
HelpSeeking_{intervention}
\le
HelpSeeking_{control}
\]

with adequate power, follow-up and service availability, the causal gateway claim weakens substantially.

---

## Verdict

**RETAIN / NARROW — Moderate evidence for a relationship-help-seeking gateway; broader family-support gateway remains OPEN.**

---

# 2. Minimum support when temporal feasibility is low

## Working relation before attack

\[
LowTemporalFeasibility
\Rightarrow
MinimumSufficientFunctions
\]

Candidate bundle:

\[
Orientation
+
Safety
+
Navigation
+
RedFlagRecognition
+
ReturnRoute
\]

---

## Positive evidence

### Dose matters in marriage/relationship education

Hawkins et al. coded 148 evaluation reports.

In controlled studies:

- low dose = 1–8 contact hours;
- moderate dose = 9–20 hours;
- high dose = 21+ hours.

Moderate-dose programmes had stronger effects than low-dose programmes on communication and relationship quality.

The authors concluded that fewer than 9 contact hours may be insufficient for the typical communication/relationship-quality outcomes examined.

Source:

- Hawkins AJ, Stanley SM, Blanchard VL, Albright M. *Exploring Programmatic Moderators of the Effectiveness of Marriage and Relationship Education Programs: A Meta-Analytic Study*. Behav Ther. 2012;43(1):77–87. DOI: 10.1016/j.beth.2010.12.006. PMID: 22304880.

### Brief/flexible interventions can still help selected couples

Halford et al. randomized 182 couples. Couples with initially low relationship satisfaction receiving a flexible relationship-education programme showed a moderate immediate increase in satisfaction relative to controls (\(d=0.50\)); highly satisfied couples showed little change.

Source:

- Halford WK et al. *Immediate effect of couple relationship education on low-satisfaction couples*. Behav Ther. 2015. PMID: 25892175.

### WHO preconception care rejects a single narrow domain

WHO's preconception-care package spans 13 domains and emphasizes adaptation to country priorities, system context, feasibility, acceptability, coverage and cost.

Sources:

- WHO. *Preconception care to reduce maternal and childhood mortality and morbidity*. 2013.
- WHO. *Preconception care: Maximizing the gains for maternal and child health*. 2013.
- WHO. *Reframing care and services to improve preconception health*. 2025 meeting report.

---

## Strongest attack

The evidence does **not** identify the project's five-item bundle as the minimum sufficient package.

In fact, relationship-education dose evidence warns that very low-dose intervention can be weaker for communication and relationship-quality outcomes.

Also:

\[
MinimumForOrientation
\neq
MinimumForRelationshipSkill
\neq
MinimumForPreconceptionHealth
\neq
MinimumForSafety
\]

There is no single empirically established minimum that covers all these functions.

---

## Critical correction

Replace:

\[
MinimumSufficientSupport
\]

with:

\[
MinimumNonDeferrableFunctions
\]

The claim is no longer:

> “This short bundle is sufficient.”

It becomes:

> “When time feasibility is low, which functions are unsafe or costly to defer, and which can be shifted to later modules without measurable loss?”

This converts an unsupported package claim into a testable timing/allocation problem.

---

## Revised relation

For function \(k\):

\[
Defer_k
\in
\{0,1\}
\]

Choose deferral by minimizing:

\[
ExpectedLoss_k(Delay)
+
DeliveryBurden_k(Now)
\]

subject to:

\[
Safety_k\ge s^*
\]

and any time-critical clinical/legal constraint.

Thus a rapid contact should not promise deep relationship-skill acquisition.

Its candidate non-deferrable functions must be tested separately.

---

## Prospective tests

Compare:

1. full programme before marriage;
2. short pre-marriage orientation + scheduled/triggered later modules;
3. short orientation + user-initiated return;
4. information-only control where ethical.

Outcomes must be function-specific:

- navigation knowledge;
- referral completion;
- safety literacy;
- communication skill;
- relationship quality;
- reproductive-health action;
- retention;
- cost.

---

## Kill criterion

If deferring candidate modules consistently worsens clinically/socially meaningful outcomes beyond an acceptable margin, those modules cannot be deferred.

If the five candidate functions do not outperform a simpler information-only contact, collapse the bundle.

---

## Verdict

**OPEN — the exact “minimum sufficient” package does not survive. Retain only the question of minimum non-deferrable functions under time constraints.**

---

# 3. Couple-visible plus private individual access

## Working relation

\[
SharedCoupleChannel
+
PrivateIndividualChannel
\]

versus:

\[
SharedCoupleChannelOnly
\]

---

## Positive evidence

### ACOG: screening must be private

ACOG's IPV guidance states that screening should occur in a private and safe setting with the patient alone, not with a partner, family member, friend or caregiver.

ACOG's reproductive-coercion guidance also recommends private spaces where conversations cannot be overheard and routinizing time alone with the patient.

Sources:

- ACOG. *Intimate Partner Violence*. Committee Opinion.
- ACOG. *Reproductive and Sexual Coercion*. Committee Opinion No. 554.

### Prepregnancy relevance

ACOG's prepregnancy counselling guidance states that IPV screening should occur during prepregnancy counselling and that privacy/confidentiality are important components.

Source:

- ACOG. *Prepregnancy Counseling*. Committee Opinion, 2019.

### WHO survivor-centred care

WHO's clinical handbook and guidelines for IPV/sexual violence emphasize first-line emotional support, safety needs, ongoing support, confidentiality and appropriate referral.

Sources:

- WHO. *Responding to intimate partner violence and sexual violence against women*. 2013.
- WHO. *Health care for women subjected to intimate partner violence or sexual violence: a clinical handbook*. 2014.

### Reproductive coercion evidence

Systematic reviews show that reproductive coercion can involve contraception sabotage, pregnancy coercion and control of pregnancy outcomes, and may be perpetrated by partners or, in international settings, family members/in-laws.

Sources:

- Grace KT, Anderson JC. *Reproductive Coercion: A Systematic Review*. Trauma Violence Abuse. 2018.
- Grace KT, Fleming C. *A Systematic Review of Reproductive Coercion in International Settings*. World Med Health Policy. 2016.

---

## Counterevidence / necessary nuance

Private access should not be interpreted as:

\[
CoupleWork = Unsafe
\]

in all cases.

A systematic review/meta-analysis of six studies found preliminary/moderate evidence that conjoint couple therapy can reduce violence in **selected mild-to-moderate situational IPV**, with careful assessment and safety precautions.

It should not be generalized to severe coercive control/intimate terrorism.

Sources:

- Karakurt G et al. *Couple Therapy for Intimate Partner Violence: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis*. J Marital Fam Ther. 2016;42(4):567–583. DOI: 10.1111/jmft.12178.
- McCollum EE, Stith SM. *Couples treatment for interpersonal violence*. Violence Vict. 2008.

---

## What survives

The strongest architecture is not:

\[
PrivateOnly
\]

and not:

\[
CoupleOnly
\]

but:

\[
SharedWhenSafe
+
PrivateRouteAlwaysAvailable
+
RiskBasedEscalation
\]

where private access is a structural option even if the main programme is couple-based.

This architecture avoids forcing a disclosure in front of the person who may be creating risk.

---

## Revised equation

\[
A^{safe}_{i,t}
=
\mathcal S(
A^{shared}_{i,t},
A^{private}_{i,t},
Risk_{i,t}
)
\]

with:

\[
A^{private}_{i,t}>0
\]

as a safety-design requirement for contexts where partner/family coercion is plausible.

When:

\[
Risk>r^*
\]

the objective changes from relationship skill-building to protected specialist response.

---

## Boundary

Most direct evidence comes from clinical IPV/reproductive-health settings, not universal premarital programmes.

Therefore the paper should call this a **safeguarding design principle extrapolated to premarital/family-transition settings**, not a directly proven premarital intervention effect.

---

## Kill criterion

The principle should be redesigned if private-channel implementation:

- increases discovery/retaliation risk;
- violates confidentiality;
- cannot route risk safely;
- creates false assurance without trained referral capacity.

---

## Verdict

**RETAIN / NARROW — Strong support for private individual access in IPV/reproductive-coercion assessment; moderate support for the dual-channel architecture as a premarital safeguarding design.**

---

# 4. Known return route plus triggered re-contact

## Working relation

\[
KnownReturnRoute
+
TransitionTriggeredContact
\]

instead of:

\[
FrontLoadEverything
\]

or:

\[
ContinuousUniversalFollowup
\]

---

## Positive evidence stream 1 — premarital education and later help-seeking

The 2014 and 2018 studies above provide a plausible “gateway” precedent: premarital education is associated with later use of couple counselling.

This supports the possibility that early contact can alter later service use.

It does not prove that a deliberately designed return route causes that effect.

---

## Positive evidence stream 2 — recurrent relationship checkups

### US RCT

215 couples were randomized to Marriage Checkup or waitlist. Checkups occurred near baseline and one year later.

Significant between-group effects were observed for intimacy, acceptance and relationship distress over portions of the two-year follow-up.

Source:

- Cordova JV et al. *The Marriage Checkup: A Randomized Controlled Trial of Annual Relationship Health Checkups*. J Consult Clin Psychol. 2014;82(4):592–604. PMID: 24932565.

### Denmark RCT

233 couples were randomized to two Marriage Checkups versus waitlist over 54 weeks.

After the second checkup, small-to-medium intervention effects appeared across all four measured relationship-health outcomes.

Source:

- Trillingsgaard T et al. *A randomized controlled trial of the Marriage Checkup adapted for private practice*. J Consult Clin Psychol. 2016;84(12):1145–1152. PMID: 27599227.

### Five-year booster counterexample

A responder subsample of 63 couples was randomized to a third Marriage Checkup or control around year 5.

There were no significant between-group effects after randomization, although within-group improvements occurred for some outcomes.

Source:

- Leth-Nissen AB et al. *A randomized controlled trial of a 5-year marriage checkup booster session*. J Marital Fam Ther. 2023;49(1):49–73. PMID: 36153652.

Thus:

\[
MoreRepeatedContact
\neq
AlwaysMoreBenefit
\]

---

## Positive evidence stream 3 — support across transition to parenthood

A meta-analysis of 21 controlled couple-focused interventions found:

- small effects on communication (\(d=.28\));
- small effects on psychological well-being (\(d=.21\));
- very small effects on couple adjustment (\(d=.09\));

with stronger effects when programmes:

- had more than five sessions;
- included both antenatal and postnatal components;
- were led by professionals.

Source:

- Pinquart M, Teubert D. *A Meta-analytic Study of Couple Interventions During the Transition to Parenthood*. Fam Relat. 2010.

Family Foundations provides a direct example of a programme spanning the transition. A replication RCT randomized 399 couples to a nine-session intervention with five prenatal and four postnatal sessions; intervention effects appeared across many coparenting, mental-health, parenting, child-adjustment and family-violence measures.

Source:

- Feinberg ME et al. *Couple-Focused Prevention at the Transition to Parenthood, a Randomized Trial*. Prev Sci / PMC10351206.

---

## Strongest attack

None of these studies directly tests:

\[
KnownReturnRoute
+
NeedTriggeredContact
\]

against:

\[
ScheduledUniversalFollowup
\]

on both outcomes **and cost**.

The Marriage Checkup evidence tests scheduled repeated contact, not a low-cost passive route-back architecture.

The transition-to-parenthood evidence often uses relatively intensive programmes.

Therefore:

\[
EvidenceForContinuity
\neq
EvidenceForTriggeredLowCostReentry
\]

---

## Revised relation

Separate three mechanisms:

\[
R_1=UserInitiatedReturn
\]

\[
R_2=TransitionTriggeredOffer
\]

\[
R_3=ScheduledPeriodicCheckup
\]

Then compare:

\[
Effect(R_k),\ Cost(R_k),\ Reach(R_k),\ Equity(R_k),\ Safety(R_k)
\]

Do not collapse them into one “re-entry” construct.

---

## Critical equity attack

User-initiated return may preferentially serve people with:

- higher literacy;
- lower stigma;
- more agency;
- more digital access;
- better memory/navigation.

Thus the cheapest model may worsen inequity.

A trigger-based or periodic invitation may be needed for people least able to self-refer.

---

## Prospective trial

Randomize or pragmatically compare:

1. user-initiated route only;
2. transition-triggered message/contact;
3. periodic checkup;
4. hybrid.

Primary endpoints:

- appropriate service uptake;
- time to help;
- relationship/family/caregiver outcome;
- safety events;
- subgroup reach;
- cost per appropriate completed support episode.

---

## Kill criterion

If user/transition-triggered contact:

- misses high-need groups;
- produces worse outcomes;
- does not reduce cost;
- or creates excessive delayed help

relative to periodic follow-up, the low-cost return architecture fails.

---

## Verdict

**OPEN / PROMISING — continuity and repeated contact have empirical support, but the specific low-cost triggered-return model remains untested.**

---

# 5. Missing Edge vs Missing Node vs Missing Capacity

## Working relation

\[
Bottleneck
\in
\{
MissingEdge,\,
MissingNode,\,
MissingCapacity,\,
Mixed
\}
\]

followed by:

\[
a^*
=
\arg\max_{a\in\{Edge,Node,Hybrid\}}
\frac{\Delta ValuedOutcome(a)}{\Delta Cost(a)}
\]

---

## Positive evidence — integrated care

A systematic review/meta-analysis of 34 integrated-care economic evaluations found overall:

\[
CostRatio\approx0.94
\]

and:

\[
OutcomeRatio\approx1.06
\]

relative to control.

Effects were stronger in studies lasting more than 12 months, but heterogeneity was substantial and average study quality was moderate.

Source:

- Rocks S et al. *Cost and effects of integrated care: a systematic literature review and meta-analysis*. Eur J Health Econ. 2020. PMID: 32632820.

This supports:

\[
Integration
\text{ can be economically favourable}
\]

but not:

\[
Integration
\text{ is always the cheapest solution}
\]

---

## Negative evidence — link-worker/social-prescribing example

A systematic review of social-prescribing link workers included eight controlled studies (n=6,500).

Findings included:

- no consistent HRQoL benefit;
- most mental-health studies showed no effect;
- certainty low/very low;
- no eligible cost-effectiveness analyses were identified.

Source:

- Kiely B et al. *Effect of social prescribing link workers on health outcomes and costs*. BMJ Open. 2022;12:e062951. PMID: 36253037.

This is a direct warning that a seemingly low-cost connecting role can be widely implemented before cost-effectiveness is known.

---

## Capacity evidence

Operations research in integrated outpatient care shows that capacity allocation affects waiting time, and urgency-blind first-come-first-served allocation can leave urgent patients waiting longer than less urgent patients.

Source:

- Deglise-Hawkinson J et al. *A Capacity Allocation Planning Model for Integrated Care and Access Management*. Prod Oper Manag. 2018. PMID: 30930608.

This supports the structural need to include:

\[
Capacity
\]

and:

\[
Urgency
\]

inside service-network design.

---

## Governance attack

A graph of organizations is not a functioning network by itself.

Network-governance theory distinguishes different modes of governance and argues that their effectiveness depends on network conditions.

Source:

- Provan KG, Kenis P. *Modes of Network Governance: Structure, Management, and Effectiveness*. J Public Adm Res Theory. 2008;18(2):229–252.

Therefore the cost of an edge must include:

\[
C_{edge}
=
Navigation
+
Coordination
+
Governance
+
Data/Information
+
Supervision
+
ReferralMaintenance
\]

not just the price of a website, coordinator or QR code.

---

## Strongest attack on the current classification

Real systems may not permit clean classification.

For example:

- a specialist service exists but has the wrong opening hours;
- nominal capacity exists but not in the needed language;
- referral succeeds but quality is poor;
- adding navigation induces demand;
- the receiving service adapts its throughput.

Thus:

\[
Edge,\ Node,\ Capacity
\]

are analytically separable but dynamically coupled.

---

## Revised bottleneck state

For service \(j\), define:

\[
B_j
=
\langle
Availability_j,\,
Connectivity_j,\,
Capacity_j,\,
Quality_j,\,
Governance_j
\rangle
\]

with incoming demand:

\[
\lambda'_j
=
\lambda_j+\Delta\lambda_j^{connection}
\]

and usable capacity:

\[
\mu^{usable}_j
=
\mu_j
\times
Quality_j
\times
Accessibility_j
\]

This multiplicative expression is only a conceptual diagnostic, not an estimated law.

Capacity slack:

\[
Slack_j
=
\mu^{usable}_j-\lambda'_j
\]

A connection intervention can reveal demand and change the state it is trying to fix.

---

## Economic decision rule

Do not optimize only:

\[
\frac{\Delta Outcome}{\Delta Cost}
\]

if outcomes are multidimensional and safety/equity are non-compensable.

Use constrained comparison:

\[
a^*
=
\arg\max_a
Value(\Delta Y_a)
\]

subject to:

\[
Budget\le B
\]

\[
Safety\ge s^*
\]

\[
Equity\ge e^*
\]

\[
Quality\ge q^*
\]

\[
WaitTime\le w^*
\]

where:

\[
a\in
\{
Edge,\,
Node,\,
Capacity,\,
Governance,\,
Hybrid
\}
\]

This is closer to multi-criteria decision analysis than to a single cost-effectiveness ratio when outcomes cannot legitimately be collapsed into one unit.

---

## Prospective falsifier

Perform bottleneck diagnosis before intervention.

If the classification predicts:

\[
BestAction_{predicted}
\]

but repeated trials show another intervention class consistently produces superior outcome/cost/safety performance, the diagnostic rule fails.

---

## Verdict

**RETAIN / NARROW — Strong rationale for capacity-aware bottleneck diagnosis; no evidence yet that this particular classification/decision rule is a validated general family-service algorithm.**

# 8. Strongest remaining research programme

After Round 3, the most informative empirical study is no longer “Does premarital education work?”

It is a sequential systems experiment.

## Stage A — transition-contact test

At a naturally occurring family-transition contact, randomize a light navigation/safety/return-access component.

Test:

\[
TransitionContact
\rightarrow
LaterAppropriateHelpSeeking
\]

## Stage B — timing test

Compare:

\[
FullBefore
\]

versus:

\[
NonDeferrableBefore + ModulesWhenRelevant
\]

## Stage C — access-channel test

Test whether adding private individual access improves safe disclosure/referral without harming participation.

## Stage D — return-mode test

Compare:

\[
UserInitiated
\quad vs \quad
Triggered
\quad vs \quad
Periodic
\]

## Stage E — system bottleneck test

Before referral expansion, classify receiving services by:

\[
Availability,\ Connectivity,\ Capacity,\ Quality,\ Governance
\]

then test whether the classification predicts which investment produces the best constrained value.

If these tests fail, the visible conceptual architecture should shrink again.
