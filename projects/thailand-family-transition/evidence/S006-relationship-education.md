# S006 evidence note — Marriage and relationship education evidence

## Source identity

This node summarizes selected meta-analytic and systematic-review evidence on marriage/relationship education (MRE) and online couple relationship education (CRE).

Primary sources reviewed for this node:

1. Hawkins AJ, Blanchard VL, Baldwin SA, Fawcett EB. *Does marriage and relationship education work? A meta-analytic study.* Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology. 2008.
2. Blanchard VL, Hawkins AJ, Baldwin SA, Fawcett EB. *Investigating the effects of marriage and relationship education on couples' communication skills: a meta-analytic study.* Journal of Family Psychology. 2009.
3. Fawcett EB et al. *Do Premarital Education Programs Really Work? A Meta-analytic Study.* Family Relations. 2010.
4. Hawkins AJ et al. *Exploring Programmatic Moderators of the Effectiveness of Marriage and Relationship Education Programs.* Behavior Therapy. 2012.
5. Spencer CM, Anderson JR. *Online relationship education programs improve individual and relationship functioning: a meta-analytic review.* Journal of Marital and Family Therapy. 2021.
6. Megale A, Peterson E, Friedlander ML. *How Effective is Online Couple Relationship Education? A Systematic Meta-Content Review.* Contemporary Family Therapy. 2022.

## Main synthesis

### What the evidence supports reasonably well

Across the MRE literature, communication outcomes are among the more consistently improved outcomes.

Online relationship education has also shown improvements across several relationship outcomes, including:

- relationship satisfaction
- communication
- confidence
- positive relationship qualities

Some online studies also report improvements in individual outcomes such as anxiety, depressive symptoms, health satisfaction, or quality of life.

### What the evidence does not support uniformly

Premarital education does **not** show uniformly strong effects across all outcomes.

The premarital-specific meta-analysis reported a mixed pattern:

- communication outcomes appeared more favorable
- relationship quality/satisfaction effects were not consistently robust when unpublished evidence was included
- longer-term prevention effects were under-studied

Earlier MRE meta-analysis also noted that outcomes such as aggression and relationship stability were rarely assessed.

### Delivery and dose

A 2012 meta-analysis found moderate-dose MRE programmes (9–20 contact hours) were associated with stronger effects than lower-dose programmes in the coded evidence.

However, this should **not** be converted into a rule that every couple must complete 9–20 hours before marriage. That evidence concerns average programme effects, while access burden and urgency vary by population and context.

Online delivery can reduce logistical barriers and has empirical support, but evidence also varies by programme and follow-up period.

## Relevance to Thailand family-transition project

**Supports**

- communication skills are a defensible module
- modular and online delivery are plausible delivery options
- relationship education should be evaluated by outcomes, not course completion alone
- effects are heterogeneous enough that a one-size-fits-all mandatory course is not evidence-based

**Does not directly support**

- that premarital education raises TFR
- that all couples benefit equally
- that more hours are always better
- that a religious, civil, or health-system setting is intrinsically superior
- that relationship education should replace therapy, health care, or safety services

## Design implication

The evidence is compatible with a tiered model:

```text
brief universal orientation
    +
optional deeper modules
    +
persistent re-entry
    +
human support when needed
```

This remains a **design synthesis**, not a proven package.
