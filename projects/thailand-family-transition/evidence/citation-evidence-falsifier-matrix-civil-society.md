# Citation–Evidence–Falsifier Matrix: minimum-cost civil-society ecosystem

## Purpose

This matrix stress-tests the minimum-cost civil-society proposal claim by claim.

Status labels:

- **Strong** — directly supported by systematic review/meta-analysis/guideline or robust comparative evidence.
- **Moderate** — supported, but generalisation or causal interpretation is limited.
- **Weak / open** — plausible but direct evidence is sparse.
- **Contradicted / revised** — current evidence does not support the earlier wording; claim must be narrowed.

---

## M1 — Formal availability is not the same as usable access

**Claim**

\[
FormalAvailability \neq EffectiveAccess
\]

**Seminal / foundational sources**

- Penchansky R, Thomas JW. *The concept of access: definition and relationship to consumer satisfaction*. Medical Care. 1981.
- Levesque JF, Harris MF, Russell G. *Patient-centred access to health care: conceptualising access at the interface of health systems and populations*. International Journal for Equity in Health. 2013.

**Current synthesis**

- Administrative-burden systematic review: learning, compliance and psychological costs can impede programme access.

**Direct support**

Access is multidimensional; discovering, reaching, paying for, engaging with and navigating services are analytically separable from nominal availability.

**Does not support**

It does not establish that the proposed civil-society interface will improve every family outcome.

**Counterevidence / alternative explanation**

In some settings the main bottleneck may be service capacity rather than navigation.

**Boundary**

If adequate specialist capacity is absent, improving access alone can increase unmet demand without improving outcomes.

**Observable implication**

People may report high formal service availability but low take-up, long delays or failed referrals.

**Falsifier**

If formal availability almost fully predicts actual use and outcome after accounting for eligibility, the extra access variables add little explanatory value.

**Status: Strong**

## M2 — Administrative burden can reduce take-up

**Claim**

\[
LearningCost + ComplianceCost + PsychologicalCost
\rightarrow
lower\ take\text{-}up
\]

**Sources**

- Moynihan D, Herd P, Harvey H. *Administrative Burden: Learning, Psychological, and Compliance Costs in Citizen-State Interactions*. JPART. 2015.
- *Administrative Burden in Citizen–State Interactions: A Systematic Literature Review*. JPART. 2024.

**Direct support**

Administrative burden literature consistently separates learning, compliance and psychological costs and documents consequences for access and inequality.

**Application**

A verified map, clear eligibility information and navigation assistance can reasonably be tested as burden-reduction tools.

**Does not support**

No direct evidence yet shows that this specific package reduces burden in Thai premarital/family services.

**Falsifier**

Navigation tools fail to reduce time, drop-off, wrong-door referrals or perceived burden.

**Status: Strong for the mechanism; open for this application**

## M3 — Warm referral may improve service receipt, but evidence is limited

**Claim to test**

\[
WarmReferral > Signposting
\]

**Source**

- *Warm Handoffs for Improving Client Receipt of Services: A Systematic Review* (2021).

**Evidence**

Only five studies met inclusion criteria; three showed increased service receipt with warm handoffs.

**Important correction**

The evidence base is too small to assert a general hierarchy in which warm referral is always superior.

**Revised wording**

Use **progressive referral intensity**:

\[
Information
\rightarrow
Signposting
\rightarrow
WarmReferral
\rightarrow
AccompaniedReferral
\]

with escalation based on risk, literacy, complexity and prior referral failure.

**Falsifier**

Warm handoffs do not improve completed referral, or their additional cost exceeds benefit in low-risk cases.

**Status: Moderate / limited**

---

## M4 — Community workers can improve some access/use outcomes, but low cost is not guaranteed

**Sources**

- Systematic review of CHW performance interventions in LMICs (2017).
- Systematic review of CHW navigation in chronic disease (2021).
- Global systematic review of CHW cost-effectiveness in primary care (2025).
- Scoping review of RMNCH CHW costs and cost-effectiveness in LMICs (2026).

**Direct support**

CHW/community-worker interventions can improve service use and selected behavioural/process outcomes. Many economic evaluations report cost-effective scenarios.

**Counterevidence / limitations**

- clinical outcomes are less consistently improved;
- navigation cost-effectiveness is insufficiently established in several domains;
- costs vary widely;
- affordability is often not assessed;
- salary/compensation reporting is inconsistent;
- equity effects are not guaranteed.

**Implication**

Community delivery belongs in the candidate solution set, but:

\[
CommunityWorker \neq AutomaticallyLowCost
\]

and:

\[
CostEffective \neq Affordable
\]

**Falsifier**

Full economic evaluation shows supervision, compensation and coordination costs remove the expected savings.

**Status: Moderate**

---

## M5 — Civil society should count labour, supervision and safeguarding costs

**Claim**

\[
C_{community}
=
Training + Supervision + Coordination + Safeguarding + Compensation
\]

**Evidence**

CHW performance and economic reviews repeatedly identify training, supervision, incentives/compensation and equipment/organisational support as relevant programme components and cost drivers.

**Does not support**

The exact additive equation is a costing template, not an empirically estimated law.

**Falsifier**

Economic evaluation shows some components are irrelevant or materially double-counted.

**Status: Strong as a costing discipline; open as exact form**

## M6 — Faith/community institutions can be delivery or contact sites, but trust-to-uptake causality is not established

**Sources**

- Systematic review of health programmes in faith-based organisations (2004).
- Annual Review of Public Health review of church-based interventions (2007).
- Systematic review of church-based interventions among Latinos (2019).
- Systematic review of faith community nursing interventions (2021).
- Systematic review/meta-analysis of church-based cardiovascular interventions (2023).
- Singapore official Bersamamu materials (2023–2026).

**Direct support**

Faith institutions have been used as health-promotion settings and can reach communities. Singapore demonstrates that Muslim religious marriage officials can provide pre/post-marriage contact and referral to national agencies.

**Does not support**

- that religious trust automatically improves service uptake;
- that mosque/church delivery is preferable to secular entry;
- that the Singapore design causes better marriage or child outcomes;
- that Christian and Muslim systems function identically.

**Revised claim**

Treat religious/community institutions as **candidate existing entry points** whose reach, trust, safety and referral performance must be measured locally.

**Falsifier**

Users avoid the node, distrust confidentiality, or referral completion is no better than alternatives.

**Status: Moderate for feasibility; weak for causal benefit**

## M7 — Singapore provides a real institutional precedent for re-entry/continuity, not proof of effectiveness

**Official sources**

- Our Marriage Journey / ROMM. *Face-to-Face session (Bersamamu) with Kadi/Naib Kadi*.
- MSF statements on Bersamamu, Journey with You and marriage support.

**Direct support**

Current official materials state that Kadi/Naib Kadi contact begins before solemnisation and continues through the first two years of marriage, with relationship guidance and referral to national agencies when needed. MSF reported 31,000 Muslim couples had benefitted from Bersamamu since launch by 2025.

**Does not support**

- causal improvement in marriage quality;
- child outcomes;
- fertility outcomes;
- cost-effectiveness.

**Use**

Institutional precedent for:

\[
PremaritalContact \rightarrow PostmaritalContinuity
\]

**Falsifier**

Outcome evaluation shows no added value or unacceptable burden relative to ordinary services.

**Status: Strong precedent; effectiveness open**

## M8 — Safe and confidential environments facilitate disclosure of domestic violence

**Sources**

- WHO clinical handbook on intimate-partner/sexual violence.
- *Barriers and facilitators of disclosing domestic violence to the healthcare service: A systematic review of qualitative research* (2021).

**Direct support**

The qualitative systematic review of 34 studies found perceived safety and concerns about consequences were disclosure barriers, while a safe and confidential environment and positive professional relationship facilitated disclosure.

**Application**

Supports operationalising **พื้นที่ปลอดภัย** with privacy/confidentiality and an individual route.

**Does not support**

- universal screening design;
- every disclosure method;
- a specific premarital implementation.

**Falsifier**

Private/confidential access does not change disclosure/help-seeking in the target setting, or creates unintended safety harms.

**Status: Strong for disclosure conditions**

## M9 — Responsive caregiving is linked to child development, but effects vary by context

**Sources**

- Care for Child Development implementation/evidence review (2018).
- Updated LMIC parenting intervention meta-analysis (2021; 21 RCTs, >10,400 children).
- Responsive caregiving scoping review (2026; 138 studies).
- East Asia/Pacific home-learning and responsive-caregiving systematic review (2026).
- Caregiver sensitivity umbrella review (2026).

**Direct support**

Responsive caregiving and home learning are associated with child-development outcomes; parenting interventions in LMICs show small-to-moderate pooled effects on cognition, language and motor development.

**Counterevidence / limits**

- social-emotional pooled effects were not significant in the 2021 review;
- effect sizes are heterogeneous;
- context and caregiver/child characteristics matter;
- long-term persistence is less well established.

**Implication**

Child development should be a core endpoint, but the proposal should not promise uniform gains.

**Status: Strong with heterogeneity**

## M10 — Parenting programmes can work, but evidence-based does not mean affordable at universal scale

**Sources**

- Parenting intervention meta-analysis in LMICs (2021).
- Thailand PLH-YC costing study (2025/2026 publication record).

**Thailand costing result**

Provider cost per caregiver: approximately USD 82–195.  
Estimated one-year national budget impact: approximately USD 50–76 million.

Authors concluded that some scaling scenarios could be prohibitively expensive and suggested testing fewer sessions or hybrid implementation while maintaining quality.

**Inference**

\[
EvidenceBasedProgramme \neq AffordableUniversalProgramme
\]

**What remains open**

Whether targeted delivery plus universal low-cost navigation is more cost-effective than universal full-dose parenting support.

**Falsifier**

A universal programme produces a lower cost per meaningful child/family outcome than targeted/tiered alternatives.

**Status: Strong for cost warning; tiered solution still open**

## M11 — Critical correction: the 2026 blended Thailand parenting trial did NOT show reduced child maltreatment

**Source**

Pongpaew W et al. *Effectiveness of a blended in-person and online parenting programme in reducing violence against children in rural Thailand: a cluster randomised controlled trial*. Lancet Regional Health – Southeast Asia. 2026.

**Trial**

- 12 sub-district health-promoting hospitals;
- 240 caregivers;
- nine-week intervention;
- two brief in-person meetings + facilitated LINE group chats.

**Results**

- no significant reduction in overall child maltreatment;
- no significant reduction in physical abuse;
- emotional-abuse estimate was higher in the intervention arm, which authors note may reflect differential reporting or increased awareness;
- authors recommend refinement and longer-term/higher-risk evaluation.

**Correction**

The trial supports **feasibility of blended delivery**, not efficacy for reducing child maltreatment.

Therefore delete any claim that this study proved blended delivery reduced violence.

**Status: Contradicted / revised**

## M12 — Caregiver stress and economic pressure can affect children through family processes

**Sources**

- Masarik AS, Conger RD. *Stress and child development: a review of the Family Stress Model* (2017).
- related family-stress literature using longitudinal designs.

**Direct support**

Economic hardship can operate through economic pressure, caregiver distress, couple/family processes and parenting to affect child outcomes.

**Use**

Supports separating:

\[
Knowledge \neq CapacityToProvideNurturingCare
\]

and including caregiver stress/load in the system.

**Does not support**

The Toledo repair equation is not a measured family-energy conservation law.

**Revised use**

Use the repair ledger as a **formal discipline / analogy**, while empirical claims are carried by family-stress, caregiving and time-poverty literature.

**Status: Strong for mediated family-stress pathways; equation form remains conceptual**

## M13 — Continuity can matter, but generic medical continuity cannot simply be imported into family-support re-entry

**Sources**

- Haggerty et al. continuity-of-care framework.
- systematic review associating doctor continuity with mortality and other healthcare outcomes.

**Direct support**

Continuity has measurable value in healthcare and is distinguishable from mere organisational availability.

**Does not support**

It does not directly prove that post-marriage re-entry into family-support systems improves family or child outcomes.

**Status**

Use continuity literature for mechanism lineage; treat premarital-to-later-family re-entry as an open empirical question.

**Status: Moderate lineage; direct application weak/open**

---

## M14 — Just-in-time/adaptive delivery supports timing-sensitive design as a nearby literature, not direct proof

**Sources**

- 2024 systematic review of just-in-time adaptive interventions.
- 2025/2026 systematic reviews/meta-analyses in mental/behavioural health.

**Direct support**

Timing and context can be used to adapt intervention content/intensity; some outcomes are promising.

**Limitations**

- many studies have weak or heterogeneous quality;
- engagement is difficult;
- evidence is mostly health/digital behaviour, not marriage preparation.

**Use**

A nearby design literature for testing:

\[
FixedDoseForAll
\quad vs \quad
TimingSensitiveSupport
\]

**Status: Weak-to-moderate analogy**

# Immediate revisions required in the main proposal

1. Change the warm-referral ladder from an assumed effectiveness hierarchy to a **progressive intensity ladder to test**.
2. Remove the claim that the 2026 Thai blended trial reduced violence.
3. Treat faith-based/community trust as an empirical variable, not an assumed benefit.
4. Treat re-entry as a design hypothesis supported by continuity precedent, not direct family-outcome evidence.
5. Do not claim community workers are inherently cheap; cost, affordability, supervision and equity must be measured.
6. Keep the “edges before nodes” rule as a **diagnostic sequence**, not a proven economic theorem.
7. Preserve child well-being and caregiver well-being as endpoints because their evidence base is substantially stronger than the direct fertility link.
