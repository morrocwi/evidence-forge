# Simulated Peer Review Round 1
## Social Sciences, Humanities, Nursing, and Culture

**Manuscript reviewed:** \`projects/thailand-family-transition/manuscript/manuscript.md\`  
**Review purpose:** adversarial editorial hardening before submission  
**Review status:** simulated disciplinary review, not external journal peer review  
**Overall editorial recommendation:** **Major Revision**

---

# Executive editorial assessment

The manuscript has a clear focal object—**premarital**—and a defensible central move: it shifts attention from premarital education as a content package to premarital as a possible **upstream access/conversion interface** within a wider family-support ecosystem.

Its strongest features are:

- refusal to equate low fertility with a mandate to maximize births;
- explicit separation of service provision, effective access, capability, safety, and downstream outcomes;
- use of Singapore as an institutional precedent rather than a policy template;
- explicit safeguarding and private-access logic;
- cultural non-collapse:
  \[
  Culture\neq Religion
  \]
  and
  \[
  CulturalFit\neq CulturalConformity
  \]
- falsifiable propositions and willingness to abandon or narrow the architecture;
- recognition that service integration can expose latent demand and therefore create capacity pressure.

The main weakness is not lack of ideas but **too many intellectual layers competing for the centre of the paper**. At present the manuscript simultaneously carries demography, policy studies, family sociology, relationship science, public health, capability theory, life-course theory, phenomenology, cultural adaptation, reproductive justice, implementation science, integrated care, network governance, economics, child development, and civil society.

For a Mahidol-style academic article, the manuscript should not attempt to make all of these co-equal theoretical pillars. The final paper needs one visible argument, with the rest functioning as supporting literatures.

The strongest visible spine is:

\[
\boxed{
Premarital\ Contact
\rightarrow
Conversion\ Conditions
\rightarrow
Family\ Capability
\rightarrow
Appropriate\ Support/Trajectory
}
\]

under the constraints:

\[
Safety,\ Autonomy,\ Culture/Meaning,\ Time,\ ServiceCapacity
\]

Child well-being and fertility should remain downstream implications, not parallel centres of the article.

# Reviewer 1 — Social Sciences

## Overall assessment

The manuscript is intellectually ambitious and has a strong sociological intuition: formal services and formal rights do not automatically become usable opportunities. The move from “programme content” to “institutional conversion” is potentially valuable.

However, the manuscript currently oscillates between three units of analysis:

\[
Individual/Couple
\leftrightarrow
Family
\leftrightarrow
ServiceSystem
\]

and occasionally jumps to population fertility without always making the level transition explicit.

**Recommendation:** Major Revision.

---

## Major comment 1 — Define the sociological object more precisely

The current definition of premarital as “the period before marriage” is still too administratively anchored.

A social-science reviewer will ask:

- Is premarital defined by legal registration?
- religious solemnisation?
- cohabitation before registration?
- engagement?
- intention to marry?
- first marriage only?
- remarriage?
- couples who already have children?
- couples who never marry but form durable families?

The sentence that describes premarital as movement “from individual/couple into family life” risks assuming that family begins at marriage.

That is sociologically too narrow.

### Required revision

Define premarital as a **relationship-to-institution transition zone**, not the universal beginning of family life.

Suggested formulation:

> Premarital refers here to the socially and institutionally recognized period in which persons or couples are preparing for a marriage event or formal marital union and may encounter marriage-related civil, health, community, cultural, religious, or digital services. It does not imply that family life, co-residence, sexual relationships, parenthood, or caregiving begin only after marriage.

This would prevent:

\[
Premarital
=
PreFamily
\]

from becoming an implicit assumption.

---

## Major comment 2 — Separate analytical levels every time the argument moves upward

The manuscript correctly states that fertility is distal, but several passages still move quickly from couple-level intervention to national population concerns.

The article should make the hierarchy explicit:

\[
Micro:
Person/Couple
\]

\[
Meso:
Family/Community/ServiceNetwork
\]

\[
Macro:
Policy/Population
\]

Then specify which claims operate at which level.

Example:

\[
PremaritalNavigation
\rightarrow
HelpSeeking
\]

is a micro/meso claim.

\[
PremaritalArchitecture
\rightarrow
TFR
\]

is a macro claim and is currently unsupported.

### Required revision

Add a short “levels of analysis” paragraph or table.

This will make the paper more credible and prevent ecological fallacy.

---

## Major comment 3 — The paper should make inequality more central

The manuscript contains equity as a constraint, but inequality currently appears more as a guardrail than an explanatory mechanism.

A social-science framing should ask:

> Who can convert premarital resources into usable capability, and who cannot?

Relevant axes include:

- income;
- employment security;
- education;
- migration status;
- disability;
- language;
- rural/urban location;
- digital access;
- gender;
- class;
- family power;
- legal status.

The strongest sociological version of the paper is not merely:

\[
Provision\neq Access
\]

but:

\[
SameProvision
\rightarrow
UnequalConversion
\]

because conversion conditions are socially stratified.

### Required revision

Promote distributional inequality from a late “equity constraint” to part of the main argument.

A stronger equation would be:

\[
FamilyCapability_{i}
=
\Phi(
Resources_i,
ConversionConditions_i,
SocialPosition_i
)
\]

where \(SocialPosition\) is not merely another demographic control.

---

## Major comment 4 — Singapore needs to be treated comparatively, not normatively

The manuscript already says Singapore should not be copied. That is good.

But it should go one step further and ask:

> What institutional conditions make the Singapore example possible?

Relevant differences include:

- scale of the state;
- administrative centralization;
- marriage governance;
- welfare architecture;
- religious administration;
- digital state capacity;
- housing regime;
- service coordination capacity.

Without this, Singapore can still read as an implicit benchmark.

### Required revision

Use the phrase:

> “Singapore as a contrastive institutional case”

rather than only “institutional precedent”.

Then explicitly distinguish:

\[
TransferableFunction
\neq
TransferableInstitution
\]

Functions such as continuity, referral, flexible access, and premarital contact may travel more easily than institutions themselves.

---

## Major comment 5 — Clarify what counts as “family capability”

The manuscript avoids a single readiness score, which is good, but “family capability” can still become too broad to falsify.

A reviewer may ask whether it simply absorbs:

- relationship skill;
- health literacy;
- economic readiness;
- safety;
- navigation;
- agency;
- caregiving capacity.

If every desirable property becomes part of capability, the construct risks becoming tautological.

### Required revision

Define family capability as a **higher-order analytical category**, not a new psychometric variable.

For empirical studies, measure component outcomes separately.

Thus:

\[
FamilyCapability
\]

should remain conceptual, while empirical studies test:

\[
Navigation,\ SafeAccess,\ HelpSeeking,\ PreconceptionUptake,\ldots
\]

# Reviewer 2 — Humanities / Philosophy / Meaning

## Overall assessment

The manuscript contains an important humanistic correction to pronatalist and technocratic policy thinking. It recognizes that marriage, reproduction, care, and family are lived projects, not simply outputs of policy.

Its main humanistic weakness is that phenomenology and meaning currently function mainly as **supporting instruments for service design**, rather than being allowed to challenge the architecture itself.

**Recommendation:** Major Revision, but with a strong conceptual core.

---

## Major comment 1 — “Upstream” is useful but potentially too linear

The title and architecture use the metaphor “upstream”.

This helps policy readers, but it can imply:

\[
Premarital
\rightarrow
Marriage
\rightarrow
Children
\]

as a natural linear life course.

Human lives are not always sequential in that way.

People may:

- cohabit;
- have children before marriage;
- marry after long co-residence;
- remarry;
- choose childlessness;
- separate;
- enter transnational family arrangements.

### Required revision

Retain “upstream” as a service-system metaphor, but explicitly state:

> Upstream does not mean chronologically prior to all forms of family life; it refers to an earlier institutional opportunity for support relative to many later service contacts.

This matters both philosophically and sociologically.

---

## Major comment 2 — Meaning should not be reduced to acceptability

The manuscript makes an excellent distinction:

\[
CulturalFit\neq CulturalConformity
\]

But “cultural meaning” sometimes still reads as a determinant of whether people accept or use a service.

A humanistic reading asks a deeper question:

> What kind of family life does the person consider worth pursuing?

Meaning is not only an access facilitator.

It can change the **end itself**.

For one person, a meaningful family life may involve children.  
For another, no children.  
For another, obligations to parents or extended kin may dominate.  
For another, marriage may be a religious covenant rather than primarily a civil partnership.

### Required revision

Distinguish:

\[
MeaningAsConversionFactor
\]

from:

\[
MeaningAsValuedEnd
\]

This is important because capability theory also distinguishes means from valued functionings.

---

## Major comment 3 — The normative hierarchy needs to be explicit

The manuscript imposes thresholds:

\[
Safety,\ Autonomy,\ Equity,\ Meaning
\]

But it does not fully explain what happens when these values conflict.

For example:

- community belonging versus individual autonomy;
- confidentiality versus family involvement;
- couple continuity versus individual safety;
- religious obligation versus personal reproductive preference.

The article currently makes safety and autonomy effective “trumps”, but this should be stated openly and justified.

### Required revision

Add a short normative paragraph:

> Cultural responsiveness is legitimate only within non-negotiable safeguards of bodily safety, freedom from coercion, privacy appropriate to risk, and meaningful consent.

This avoids pretending all values can simply be optimized simultaneously.

---

## Major comment 4 — Phenomenology should be lighter in the final paper, but sharper

The manuscript does not need Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, and Schutz in detail.

But it should preserve the conceptual consequences:

\[
FormalAccess\neq ExperiencedAccess
\]

\[
ClockTime\neq LivedTime
\]

\[
SafetyProtocol\neq FeltSafety
\]

These are powerful because they challenge administrative descriptions of services.

### Required revision

Keep phenomenology as a **methodological sensitivity**, not a named theoretical pillar.

This fits the target article better.

---

## Major comment 5 — Avoid “quality of children”

The manuscript mostly uses “child well-being” and “nurturing care”, which is good.

Any wording that sounds like:

- “quality child”;
- “quality of children”;
- “human quality” without explanation

can carry eugenic or instrumental connotations in international humanities/social-science review.

### Required revision

Prefer:

- child well-being;
- developmental conditions;
- nurturing care;
- human development;
- opportunities for children to thrive.

When citing Thai policy documents using “quality people/human quality”, make clear that it is the source's policy language, not the manuscript's normative category.

---

# Reviewer 3 — Nursing / Family and Public Health

## Overall assessment

The manuscript is relevant to nursing because premarital and preconception contacts intersect with health promotion, reproductive health, mental health, IPV recognition, family nursing, referral, and continuity of care.

However, in its current form the article is more clearly a **social-policy/family-systems paper** than a nursing academic article.

If submitted to a nursing journal, the nursing contribution must be made more visible without artificially turning every component into a nursing task.

**Recommendation:** Major Revision.

---

## Major comment 1 — Add a dedicated nursing practice section

The current “implications” are broad.

A nursing reviewer will ask:

> What is the specific role of nurses?

Potential nursing functions supported by the manuscript include:

- preconception health education;
- fertility and reproductive-health navigation;
- assessment of health history and reproductive risk within scope;
- confidential enquiry and safe referral according to safeguarding protocol;
- mental-health recognition and referral;
- continuity across primary care, reproductive health, pregnancy, postpartum, and child/family services;
- health literacy;
- referral completion;
- community coordination.

### Required revision

Add a section titled:

## Implications for Nursing Practice and Family Health Services

Clarify that nursing does **not** replace:

- specialist violence response;
- psychotherapy;
- legal advice;
- religious counselling;
- social welfare eligibility decisions.

This strengthens professional boundaries.

---

## Major comment 2 — Preconception care needs a stronger nursing bridge

The manuscript has a good preconception section but still treats it partly as an adjacent literature.

For nursing, this should be one of the strongest bridges between premarital and health-system relevance.

The paper should connect:

\[
Premarital
\rightarrow
PreconceptionHealth
\rightarrow
PrimaryCare/CommunityNursing
\]

through:

- fertility awareness;
- folic acid;
- chronic disease management;
- medication review;
- vaccination/infection risk where applicable;
- mental health;
- reproductive planning;
- referral for infertility or high-risk conditions.

### Required revision

Strengthen the point that premarital contact is not necessarily a new clinic; it can be a navigation layer into existing preconception care.

---

## Major comment 3 — Safeguarding needs implementation detail

The paper rightly states:

\[
SharedWhenSafe + PrivateRoute + RiskBasedEscalation
\]

A clinical reviewer will immediately ask:

- Who assesses risk?
- What training is required?
- What happens after disclosure?
- What is the emergency pathway?
- What is documented?
- What is not documented?
- How is confidentiality protected?
- How are digital traces handled?
- What if the receiving service is unavailable?

### Required revision

Do not design a full protocol in this conceptual article, but explicitly state:

\[
PrivateAccess
\neq
SafeSystem
\]

unless there is:

\[
Training + Protocol + ReferralCapacity + Governance
\]

This is particularly important for nursing credibility.

---

## Major comment 4 — Outcome hierarchy should be more clinically operational

The paper distinguishes proximal and distal outcomes well.

For nursing/health-system readers, make the hierarchy explicit:

### Proximal
- health literacy;
- navigation literacy;
- private-access usability;
- identification of appropriate service;
- referral initiation/completion.

### Intermediate
- preconception-care uptake;
- mental-health help-seeking;
- fertility-service uptake where indicated;
- safety-service uptake;
- relationship/caregiver outcomes.

### Distal
- pregnancy outcomes;
- caregiving conditions;
- child well-being;
- fertility/population outcomes.

This will prevent reviewers from accusing the paper of causal overreach.

---

## Major comment 5 — The Mahidol Model comparison is useful but needs a boundary

The manuscript uses Mahidol Model as a Thai precedent for area-based integration.

That is useful, but adolescent pregnancy prevention is not premarital family formation.

### Required revision

State clearly:

> The Mahidol Model is used only as evidence that multisector integration, local workforce activation, screening, and referral can be operationalized in Thailand; it is not evidence that the same intervention architecture is effective for premarital populations.

That one sentence will prevent analogy from being mistaken for direct evidence.

---

# Reviewer 4 — Culture / Cultural Studies

## Overall assessment

The manuscript makes a strong conceptual correction by refusing to equate culture with religion and by distinguishing cultural fit from cultural conformity.

This is one of the manuscript's strongest features.

The main remaining problem is that culture is still sometimes represented as a vector of attributes rather than as an **ongoing field of negotiation, power, memory, place, and belonging**.

**Recommendation:** Major Revision with preservation of the current anti-essentialist direction.

---

## Major comment 1 — Culture must not become a checklist variable

The vector:

\[
Language,\ Kinship,\ GenderNorms,\ ChildbearingMeaning,\ldots
\]

is analytically useful, but cultural studies reviewers may object if it appears to decompose culture into measurable components as though culture were a stable property of a group.

### Required revision

Add:

> These dimensions are analytical prompts rather than a fixed taxonomy of culture. Their meaning is produced and negotiated historically and relationally, and may change across situations and generations.

This prevents reification.

---

## Major comment 2 — Distinguish cultural legitimacy from cultural authority

A culturally embedded institution may be recognizable and trusted by some users while also exercising moral authority or social control.

Therefore:

\[
CulturalLegitimacy
\neq
IndividualTrust
\]

and:

\[
CommunityAuthority
\neq
PersonalConsent
\]

This is particularly important in premarital settings because marriage often activates family and community expectations.

### Required revision

The manuscript should explicitly analyze both:

- supportive cultural embeddedness;
- possible gatekeeping/coercion through the same institutions.

This is stronger than treating cultural institutions only as low-cost access nodes.

---

## Major comment 3 — Thailand needs more internal cultural plurality

The paper correctly discusses Islamic and Christian systems as examples, but the Thailand section is still nationally aggregated.

A cultural reviewer would expect explicit acknowledgment of variation across:

- central/urban and rural settings;
- Thai Buddhist majority contexts;
- Malay Muslim communities in the South;
- Christian minority communities;
- Chinese-Thai family traditions;
- migrant and transnational couples;
- interfaith/intercultural marriages;
- generational change;
- class and education.

The article does not need to describe all of these empirically, but it should state that “Thailand” is not a single cultural unit.

### Required revision

Use:

\[
Thailand
\neq
SingleCulturalContext
\]

as a conceptual boundary.

---

## Major comment 4 — Intercultural and transnational marriage should be visible

The manuscript's architecture is particularly relevant to couples crossing:

- nationality;
- language;
- religion;
- migration status;
- legal systems;
- family expectations.

Yet this population is barely visible in the current text.

### Required revision

Add intercultural/transnational couples as an example of where:

\[
Language + LegalNavigation + CulturalMeaning + FamilyNorms
\]

interact strongly.

Do not overexpand into migration studies; one paragraph is enough.

---

## Major comment 5 — Cultural adaptation must include the right to refuse culture

The manuscript already states:

\[
CulturalFit\neq CulturalConformity
\]

Take this one step further.

A culturally responsive service must allow a person to say:

> “This community norm is not my norm.”

### Required revision

Define cultural responsiveness as supporting:

\[
Recognition + Comprehensibility + Belonging + Voice
\]

while preserving:

\[
Exit + Dissent + Autonomy
\]

This is especially important when working with religious/community premarital systems.

---

# Cross-review synthesis

All four reviewers converge on the same conclusion: the manuscript is strongest when premarital is treated neither as a universal curriculum nor as the biological beginning of family life, but as a **specific institutional and social access site**.

The current argument should therefore be compressed from:

+
LowFertility
+

+

+
ChildDevelopment
+
CivilSociety

\[
\boxed{

\rightarrow

# Editorial decision

**Major Revision**

The manuscript should proceed to the next round. It should not be rejected or expanded with additional theories at this stage.

The next version should be **shorter, more socially grounded, more culturally reflexive, and more explicitly relevant to nursing practice**.

The most important editorial rule is:

\[
DoNotAddMoreTheory
\]

Instead:

\[
ClarifyObject
+
ClarifyLevels
+
ClarifyBoundaries
+
ClarifyPracticeImplications
\]

After those revisions, the manuscript will be ready for a second adversarial review focused on:

1. claim-to-citation accuracy;
2. redundancy and word count;
3. nursing-journal fit;
4. final title/abstract;
5. Vancouver bibliography verification.
